Is there enough time?

The math in the dispute over Vox Day's case against evolution, and the simulations used to check it. Same story at five depths. Pick a level; the lab bench below works at every level.

Explain like I'm

For a five-year-old

The bead jar

Imagine a big jar of beads. Almost all of them are white, but one is green. Each day you make a new jar: you reach into the old jar without looking, take out one bead, put a copy of it in the new jar, drop it back, and do that again and again until the new jar is full.

Some days you pick the green bead a lot, and some days you miss it. Most of the time the green bead just disappears. But once in a while, by luck, the jar turns all green. Scientists call that a change "sticking."

Living things are like that. Every baby gets its body's instructions copied from mom and dad, with a few tiny changes. Most changes disappear. A few stick.

The big question

Long, long ago, people and chimpanzees had the same great-great-great (keep going!) grandparents. Since then, millions of tiny changes have stuck. Some people asked: was there enough time for that many changes to stick?

One side says: not enough time

A writer named Vox Day did a big counting problem. In a science lab, germs took a long time for each change to stick. If people were that slow, he says, you would need about a million times more time than there was.S1

The other side says: the counting is off

Other scientists said: lots of changes can be on their way at the same time, like many boats on a river. Some differences were there from the start. And some were counted letter by letter when they were really one change.

So we checked everybody's homework

We did not pick a side first. Before each test we wrote down what each side expected to happen, sealed it, and then let a computer play the bead game over and over.

What we found
  • A lot of Vox Day's adding and multiplying was right.
  • One of his ideas was right too: there is a real limit on how fast helpful changes can spread when life is hard.
  • He also changed his mind on one point when people showed him a mistake. That is a good thing to do.
  • The other side was right that many changes can travel at once, and that some were counted letter by letter.
  • The other side made some small counting slips too, and one pair of numbers only matched because something was counted twice.
Still being checked

A puzzle about changes that only help when several happen together, like a lock that needs every number right. The computers are still working on it. A second, different computer program is also checking our first one.

Try the bead jar on the lab bench below. Press Play and watch the green beads.

For an eight-year-old

Game 1: the copying jar

A population is a jar of beads. Each bead is one copy of a gene. Every generation the jar is refilled by picking beads from the old jar at random, with copying. Nobody chooses. That luck is called drift.

Start with one green bead in a jar of 100. Its chance of taking over the whole jar is 1 in 100, the same as its share of the jar. Bigger jar, smaller chance. Try it with the drift jar: press "Run 1,000 trials" and count.

Game 2: the river of new changes

New changes keep arriving every generation, like paper boats dropped into a river. Most sink near the start. A few float all the way across. Here is the surprising part: in a big population more boats are dropped, but each one is less likely to make it, and the two effects cancel out. The number reaching the far bank each generation ends up the same as the copying-mistake rate, whatever the size of the population. Scientists write that as k = μ (say "k equals mew").

Game 3: the budget

Think of time as pocket money. People and chimps split about 6.3 million years ago. At 25 years per generation, that is about 252,000 generations.S4

Vox Day's budget

In a long lab experiment with germs, one change stuck about every 1,322 generations.S2 252,000 ÷ 1,322 is about 190 changes. But he counts 205 million changes needed on the human side. So he says the budget is short about a million times.S1

The critics' budget

They say changes don't wait in line one at a time: many boats float at once. Using the river rule, about 19 million single-letter changes are expected after the split, and about 15 to 20 million more differences were already in the ancestors. Together that is close to the roughly 35 million single-letter differences that were measured.S20S21

Both budgets are on the budget board. Press each side's button and see which inputs change.

For a ten-year-old

Your DNA is a book of about 3.2 billion letters (A, C, G, T).S20 When it is copied for a baby, a few letters come out wrong. That is a mutation. If every member of a species eventually carries the new letter, it has become fixed. A fixed difference between two species is a substitution.

The raffle

Imagine a raffle where every gene copy in the population holds one ticket. Each generation, the next generation's copies are drawn from the tickets. A brand-new mutation starts with one ticket out of 2N (N people, two copies each). If it is neither helpful nor harmful (neutral), its chance of winning the whole raffle in the end is exactly its share: 1 in 2N.

A helpful mutation gets a few bonus tickets each draw. Its chance rises to about 2s, where s is the size of the bonus. A 1% edge gives roughly a 2% chance. Most helpful mutations are still lost by bad luck early on. Try it in the selection race.

"a neutral substitution rate that's equal to the mutation rate"Zach Hancock, Gutsick Gibbon video, 2026-10-03, 01:48:19 (GG-03)

How the checking works

A simulation is a pretend population inside a computer. It follows the raffle rules exactly, generation after generation, so you can count what happens instead of arguing about it.

Pre-registration is like writing your guess on paper and sealing it in an envelope before the game starts. The audit writes down what each side's math predicts, saves it publicly, and only then runs the simulation. Nobody can quietly change their guess afterward.

Cross-tool replication is like asking a second referee with a different stopwatch to time the same race. If two independently written programs agree, a bug in one of them is unlikely to be the cause.

For a twelve-year-old: the simple formulas

1. Drift and the fixation chance

A population of N diploid individuals carries 2N copies of each gene. Each generation is a random sample of 2N copies from the last one (a Wright–Fisher population). A neutral new mutation starts as 1 copy out of 2N, so:

P(fix, neutral) = 1 / (2N)

2. Why population size cancels: k = μ

Let μ be the mutation rate per letter per generation. Each generation, a population makes 2Nμ new mutations at a given letter. Each one fixes with chance 1/(2N). Multiply:

k = (2N μ) × (1 / 2N) = μ

The N cancels. This is the substitution rate once the population is in its long-run steady state. Day accepted this cancellation on 2026-08-27:S5

"Supply is 2Nμ in the census N, the fixation probability of a new copy is 1/(2N) in the same N, the two correctly cancel"Day, "The Response to the Retraction", 2026-08-27, ¶2 (claim B3g)

Later he gave other values, such as k = 32.3μ, which the audit scored as contradicted.S6S29

3. Start state: an empty pipe or a full one

A neutral fixation takes about 4Ne generations to finish. If the population starts with no mutations in flight (an empty pipe), nothing fixes at first, and the count lags behind μ × T. If it starts at equilibrium (a full pipe), fixations come out at rate μ from day one. The mutation river lets you switch between the two.

Day

His September paper started with an empty pipe ("At generation zero, the subsitution pipeline is empty," typo in the original). On 2026-10-01 he revised that: "The pipeline was full, but it was much shorter."S9

Critics

The common ancestors were a real population with old variation, so the pipe was full. Mansfield: "The 'pipeline' would have been full from the X generations preceding that point in time."S9

AuditThe empty-start math is exact. But with sourced histories of the ancestral population size, the count comes out 1.9 to 4.0 times more than μ × T, not less.S16

4. Selection

A mutation with advantage s fixes with chance about 2s when 4Ns is large. The exact version is Kimura's formula (in the ELI18 view and the selection race). Both sides use it; the dispute is about time and numbers, not this formula.

5. The cost of selection (Haldane)

When a helpful gene replaces an old one, the carriers of the old one must leave fewer offspring. If the population can only spare so many extra deaths, only so many helpful changes can be paid for at once. Haldane's figure is about 1 per 300 generations, which Day quotes.S7

AuditUnder hard selection the limit is real. Its size depends on how many extra offspring a population produces (R). For R of 1.3 or more, the tested cap was 9 to 100 times faster than 1 per 300. Haldane's own setting (R around 1.1) gives about his number and has not been simulated yet.S18

6. The MITTENS budget

fixations possible = generations ÷ (generations per fixation) = 252,000 ÷ 1,322 ≈ 190

Required, per Day: 205 million, so the shortfall is about 1,075,000×.S1 The critics change three inputs: what is counted (events, not letters), the rate model (k = μ in parallel, not one at a time), and the ancestral share. Play with them on the budget board.

7. Ne versus N

N is the head count. Ne, the effective size, is the size of an ideal population that drifts as fast as the real one. Uneven family sizes and population crashes make Ne smaller than N. Ne sets how long fixation takes. For a neutral mutation, Ne does not change the long-run rate k. The audit checked both: across offspring variances it found P(fix) = 1/(2N) and fixation time ≈ 4Ne.S15

8. Waiting for several changes at once

Some features may need several mutations before they help. If each step helps a little (stepping stones), selection keeps each step and the wait grows roughly with the number of steps. If nothing helps until all are in place (a valley), the wait grows roughly like (chance per step) multiplied by itself once per step. That is much slower. The combination lock toy shows the difference.

Pending (D15)

Ola Hössjer, an ally of Day's case, predicts that the wait for several human gene-expression changes "far exceeds 9 million years." The audit reproduced his published tables, and its main sweep is still running. The two sides agree on the arithmetic; they differ on whether the real target is a valley or a path of stepping stones.S24

For an eighteen-year-old: the real formulas and the audit's numbers

Claim ids (A2, B3, H …) point to docs/research/claims/. Verdicts are listed as internal / fidelity / external, from the claim files as of 2026-10-09. These are R4 results; the R5 synthesis is not written yet.

Neutral fixation and the substitution rate

In any exchangeable neutral model with constant size 2N, a single copy's frequency is a martingale, so Pfix = 1/(2N) regardless of offspring variance. With supply 2Nμ per site per generation:

k = 2Nμ · 1/(2N) = μ (steady state, discrete generations)

The audit's B3 check measured Pfix ≈ 0.0025 = 1/(2N) at offspring variance σ² from 1 to 10.7, where Ne fell from 200 to 19. The reading "1/(2Ne)" predicts 0.00249 to 0.0268. Fixation time tfix/Ne stayed at 3.95 to 4.07.S15 So Day is right on time and wrong on probability. B3 (Day's k ≠ μ values) stands at pending / partial / contradicted.S29 One credit to Day's direction: with overlapping generations and fluctuating N (Balloux–Lehmann), k ≠ μ, but symmetric cycles give only −0.3% to −2%. Day's 0.743μ depends on the averaging window.S26

Start state

From an empty start, E[F(T)] = μL∫0T FX(t) dt, where FX is the distribution of the time to fixation; roughly μL(T − 4Ne). B1 (exact for an empty start) is holds / n/a / contested. B1c, the audit question of whether the ancestral pipe was empty, came out holds / n/a / contradicted for the empty premise: every sourced step history of Ne gives an excess K/UT = 1.9 to 4.0.S16

Divergence with ancestral polymorphism

E[d] = 2μT + 4Ne,ancμ (per site; × L for the genome)

With μ = 1.2×10⁻⁸, L = 3.2×10⁹, T = 252,000: 2μLT = 19.35M. The ancestral share is 15.6 to 20.3M (residuals against CSAC's ~35M and GAP-07b's 37.77M SNVs, or θancL at Yoo's Ne,anc = 1.32×10⁵), giving a null of 35 to 39.6M SNVs.S20S22 The critics' "38M matches" (Hancock: "about 38 million"; Nesslig20: "~37.8 million") reach that number as a post-split count with a per-generation rate of 76.8 or 75, about twice the 38.4 haploid value. That equals the true post-split 19.35M plus the ancestral term, so the match is a coincidence of two terms. On an SNV basis the critics' conclusion survives (X1 rule N3). On Hancock's own event basis it does not: adding the omitted ancestral term gives an event null of at least 58.6M against 42.07M measured, so his B5c is scored non-sequitur / unverifiable. Nesslig20's arithmetic holds, and his 75 is uncited (holds / unverifiable).S20

Selection: Kimura

u(p) = (1 − e−4Nesp) / (1 − e−4Nes), p = 1/(2N) ⇒ u ≈ 2s(Ne/N) for 4Nes ≫ 1

Conditional sweep time ≈ (2/s)ln(2Ne) is Day's form (Q45).S32 Day's (2/s)ln(2N) gives 19,807 generations at N = 10⁴, s = 0.001. Simulation gives 8,525 ± 39 and the diffusion value is 8,480, so that use overstates by 2.3× (D9).S23

MITTENS

Fmax = (tdiv · d) / (glen · Gf); 3.0: 252,000 / 1,322 ≈ 191 vs 205×10⁶ ⇒ 1,075,000×

Gf = 1,322 comes from the LTEE ("45.4 fixations, yielding a cumulative rate of 1,322 generations per fixation"). The book used 1,400 and a 2026-10-03 post 1,587; that drift is logged.S2S3 A2 is holds / unverifiable / contested: the order of magnitude reproduces, but the calibration is underdetermined.S28 The 1,075,000× factors into ≈ 11.7× (letters versus events; A3x holds / partial / supported, GAP-07b counts 42.1M events, 9.7× raw) and Day's own SNV-only 91,600×.S10S19 The rest is the transfer from an asexual LTEE to a recombining mammal. In F2, clonal interference cut throughput to Rint = 0.088 at 32 loci, while free recombination kept 0.975 at 272 loci. F1 measured 0.397 substitutions per generation while one sweep took 847 generations (latency is not throughput). F1 and F2 are both holds / n/a / contested.S17

Cost of selection

Under hard selection with reproductive excess R and per-substitution cost D (≈ ln(2N) + 1, doubled for diploid additive selection), the rate cap is λD < ln R. For R ≥ 1.3 the tested cap lay 9 to 100× above 1/300. Day's 1/300 is recovered in Haldane's regime (R ≈ 1.1, diploid D ≈ 20 to 30), which is not yet simulated. Soft selection did not remove the cost: at equal supply it ran within about 15% of hard, and slightly slower. H is holds / partial / contested.S18 Day's "at most 2 percent of the average genome" for the adaptive share is near what neutral theory also assumes. If the rest is neutral, the weight of the argument moves to branch B (the neutral clock) rather than being settled there.S8

Multi-step waiting times (D15)

Hössjer, Bechly & Gauger 2021 use a haploid Moran model, with binding sites of width W = 6 to 10, at μ = 10⁻⁸ and N = 10⁴. The audit reproduces their Tables 4, 5 and 9 (worst deviation 0.3%) and Durrett & Schmidt 2008 (8.70×10⁶ generations for a human pair).S24 If the intermediate steps are neutral or harmful (a valley), the wait grows steeply with the number of steps; if each step helps (stepping stones), the waits roughly add. The audit's reading of the status file: both sides agree on Hössjer's arithmetic and differ on what the target is.

The lab bench

These are small, honest versions of the real thing: Wright–Fisher populations with true binomial sampling, kept small so they run in your browser. The audit's own checks use the same rules at larger sizes, with predictions committed before each run. These toys are for intuition and are not the audit's results.

1 Drift jar

Green beads are the new change. Each generation the jar is refilled by blind picking. Watch the line: most green runs fall to the bottom and die out, and a few climb to the top.

Every bead is one gene copy; the jar holds 2N of them. Press "Run 1,000 trials." The share that took over lands near 1 in 2N. A bigger jar makes that smaller.

Each generation draws Binomial(2N, p). Starting at p = 1/(2N), the fraction of runs that fix converges on 1/(2N); losses are fast, while fixations take about 4N generations. Try "half and half" to see the chance equal the starting share.

2 Mutation river (substitution counter)

Little boats (new changes) are dropped in on the left. Most sink. The ones that reach the right bank have "stuck." Count how many arrive.

Each dot is one mutation; left means rare, right means everyone has it. Switch N up and down: the arrival rate stays near μ. Then compare "empty river" with "already flowing": the empty one starts slow.

Infinite-sites, unlinked neutral WF: Poisson(2Nμ) new mutations each generation, and each segregating count is redrawn as Binomial(2N, c/2N). The equilibrium start draws the neutral spectrum, Poisson(θ/i) sites with i copies, θ = 4Nμ. Here μ is per gene copy per generation (a toy region, not one site). Watch the cumulative count against the dashed μt line: the slope matches k = μ at every N, and the empty start falls short by roughly μ × 4N.

Day's reading (branch B)

The empty start is the right model at the split, so fixations lag by about 4Ne. Ne sets the time scale. He later revised this to a pipe that was "full, but much shorter."S9

Critics' reading

The ancestors were already a running river, so substitutions flow at μ from the start, and N cancels.S11S12

Audit: the empty-start formula is exact (B1 internal holds); sourced Ne histories give an excess, not a deficit (B1c).S16

3 Selection race

Now the green bead gets a tiny head start each round. It still usually loses, but it wins more often than a plain bead.

s is the head start: 0.010 means 1% more copies on average. Even helpful changes are mostly lost early. Compare the simulated bar with the "2s" rule.

Genic selection p' = p(1+s)/(1+ps), then Binomial(2N, p'). Kimura (here Ne = N): u = (1 − e−2s)/(1 − e−4Ns). When 4Ns is small, the chance collapses toward 1/(2N); when 4Ns ≫ 1 it approaches 2s. The error bar is ±2 SE.

Sliders take effect when released; each change restarts the tally with 1,500 trials.

4 MITTENS budget board

One pile is the changes needed. The other is how many the time allows. Press the two sides' buttons and see how differently they count.

Pick a side's inputs. The bars are on a "times ten" scale: each step to the right is ten times bigger. Then change one input at a time to see which one matters most.

Bars are log₁₀ from 1 to 10⁹. The models are: sequential (T/Gf, MITTENS without the d term), Haldane (T/300), and neutral (2μLT + 4NancμL, both lineages). The two presets count different things (human-lineage fixations against two-genome SNV differences); the ledger says which.

5 Combination lock illustrative · not the D15 result

Two locks need the same secret letters. On the left, every right letter clicks and stays. On the right, nothing clicks until all letters are right at once. Which opens first?

Each try changes one random dial to a random letter. "Stepping stones" keeps any dial that is already right, the way selection keeps a helpful step. "Valley" has no partial credit. Add dials and watch the valley's wait explode.

Stepping stones: the expected tries grow about like K·ln K. Valley: a random walk on 4K states, so the wait grows roughly like 4K. Real waiting-time models (Hössjer et al.; Durrett & Schmidt) add population size, drift, tunnelling and selection strength. This toy shows only why "valley versus stepping stones" is the crux.

How the audit's simulations work

  • Exact models first. Each check uses an exact Markov chain or a forward Wright–Fisher, Cannings or individual-based simulation. Textbook baselines must reproduce before any disputed number is tested. The coalescent is used only for baselines, because it assumes the equilibrium that is in dispute.
  • Pre-registration. Each script is committed with both sides' predictions in its docstring before the main run. Anything changed afterwards is committed separately and labelled "post hoc".
  • Reviews. Load-bearing checks get three reviews: correctness, a steelman for Day and a steelman for the critics.
  • Cross-tool replication (XT). Core results are re-run in an independently written simulator (fwdpy11). Agreement means a bug in one code base is unlikely to explain the result. XT is in progress.S25

Caveats: this audit was done almost entirely by AI agents (Anthropic's Claude) directed by one person, and it is not peer-reviewed. The paid book was not bought, so book-only claims are second-hand. Rhetoric on every side is tagged, not fact-checked. Corrections from either side are welcome, with a locator.
