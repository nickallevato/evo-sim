# Is there enough time?
### The whole story, for an eight-year-old

## 1. The question
Inside every living thing is a long list of instructions called **DNA**. It's like a recipe book for building a body.

When a baby is made, the recipe book gets copied. Copying is *almost* perfect, but every baby ends up with a few tiny typos. Most typos don't matter. A few hurt, and a few help.

Scientists think people and chimpanzees came from the same ancestors a very long time ago: millions of years, or hundreds of thousands of generations of grandparents. Today our recipe books are different in **millions** of small ways.

So here's the big question: **was there enough time for all those differences to pile up?**

## 2. One side says: no, not nearly enough
A writer worked out a math problem about this. It goes like this:

- Scientists have watched bacteria in a lab for over 35 years, through many thousands of generations.
- In the lab, a new typo spread to *every* bacterium only about once every **1,300 generations**.
- Humans have only had about 250,000 generations since the split. At the bacteria's speed, that's only about **190** changes.
- But we see *millions* of differences. The math says we'd need about a **million times** more time!

## 3. The other side says: that math has mistakes
Other people answered back. Here are their three biggest reasons.

**Lots of changes can spread at the same time.** Picture a long moving belt in a factory. Each box takes a long time to ride to the end. But the belt is full of boxes, so new ones keep arriving at the end.

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/conveyor.svg" width="580" alt="Conveyor belt full of boxes"></p>

**Some differences are really old.** The shared ancestors weren't all the same as each other, just like kids in your class aren't. So some of today's differences were already there before people and chimps went separate ways.

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/cousins.svg" width="580" alt="Ancestors already differed before the split"></p>

**It depends how you count.** If someone sneaks the word "BIGFAT" into "THECAT", is that one change or six? The "not enough time" math counted some big changes letter by letter.

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/typos.svg" width="580" alt="One change can add many letters"></p>

## 4. So we checked
We didn't pick a side first. We used computers to pretend to be animals having babies for thousands of generations, with typos and all, and we counted what happened. Before each test, we wrote down what each side said *should* happen, so nobody could change their answer afterwards.

## 5. What we found: both sides were partly right
<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/both-sides.svg" width="620" alt="Both sides got some things right and some wrong"></p>

- Some of the "not enough time" math was **exactly right**, but it started from a guess that wasn't true: that the ancestors had no differences at all.
- Counting big changes letter by letter made the number about **12 times** too big.
- The "math is wrong" side made mistakes too. Some people counted the same differences **twice**.
- One idea the "math is wrong" side liked for making the problem go away didn't work when we tested it.

## 6. What we still don't know
- When a helpful change spreads, how hard is that on a family, and can a slow-growing animal like us keep up? We haven't finished testing that.
- Some parts of the "not enough time" argument we haven't tested at all yet.

## 7. The most important part
This isn't about telling you what to believe. It's about **checking numbers carefully and being fair to both sides**.

Our own work hasn't been checked by outside scientists yet. That's okay. Good checking means *everyone's* work gets checked, including ours.
