# Is there enough time?
### The whole story, for a five-year-old (or someone reading to one)

## 1. The question
Every time a baby is born, its body's instructions get copied from its mom and dad. The copying is *almost* perfect, but not quite. Each baby gets a few tiny changes.

A very, very long time ago, people and chimpanzees had the same great-great-great-(keep going for a long time!)-grandparents. Since then, people and chimps have become different in **millions** of tiny ways.

Some people asked a big question: **has there been enough time for all of those changes?**

## 2. One side says: not enough time
One person did a big math problem. It went like this: *"In a science lab, bacteria made one lasting change about every 1,300 generations. If people changed that slowly, there wasn't nearly enough time."*

The answer came out like a race you could never win: you'd need about a **million times more time** than there was.

## 3. The other side says: the math has mistakes
Other people said: *"Wait, that math is wrong!"* They gave three reasons.

**Lots of changes can happen at the same time.** Think of a moving belt at a factory. Each box takes a long time to ride from one end to the other. But the belt is full of boxes, so one comes off the end every few seconds.

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/conveyor.svg" width="560" alt="A conveyor belt full of boxes"></p>

**Some differences were there from the start.** The great-great-grandparents weren't all the same as each other, just like kids in your class aren't all the same. So some of today's differences are *old*, not new.

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/cousins.svg" width="560" alt="Ancestors who already differed, splitting into two families"></p>

**Some changes were counted the wrong way.** If you add the word "BIGFAT" into "THECAT", is that one change or six? It depends how you count!

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/typos.svg" width="560" alt="Counting letters versus counting changes"></p>

## 4. So we checked the homework
We didn't pick a side first. We checked the math for **both** sides, the way a teacher checks homework. We used computers to pretend to be animals having babies for thousands and thousands of generations, and we counted what happened.

## 5. What we found
**Both sides got some things right, and both sides made mistakes.**

<p align="center"><img src="../../img/eli/both-sides.svg" width="600" alt="Both sides got some things right and some things wrong"></p>

- Some of the "not enough time" math was **exactly right**, but it started from a guess that wasn't true.
- The "not enough time" side counted some changes letter by letter, which made the number much too big.
- The "math is wrong" side counted some differences **twice**.
- One idea the "math is wrong" side liked, called "soft selection", didn't work when we tested it.

## 6. What we don't know yet
Some parts we're still checking. The biggest one: how hard is it on a family when a helpful change spreads? Nobody has finished working that out, including us.

## 7. The most important part
This isn't about telling you what to believe. It's about **checking numbers carefully and being fair to everybody**, even people you disagree with.

Grown-up scientists haven't checked *our* work yet either. That's fine; that's how it should work. Everybody's homework gets checked.
