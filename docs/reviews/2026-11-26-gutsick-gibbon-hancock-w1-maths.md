## 1. The argument

The host reads the slides' MITTENS case: 252,000 generations, 1,400 generations per fixation, 180 fixations, "204,999,820 short" of 205 million. Hancock then argues three things. First, the equation is serial: "it assumes that each mutation has to both arise and go to fixation before the next mutation can occur." (gg1@01:32:12, GG-01). Such a model predicts almost no standing variation, which real populations do not show (GG-13). Second, the count is "off by a factor of two" because both lineages fix mutations (GG-02). Third, he derives the neutral substitution rate k = μ from Kimura's fixation probability (GG-03), multiplies by the genome and by two lineages, and gets about 38 million fixed differences, close to the 35-40 million single-nucleotide differences. An edit insert corrects his genome size from diploid to haploid and keeps the 76 per generation by citing an all-variant de novo count (GG-05, GG-06). He then asks what rate 205 million would need: "something like 407" per generation, "around five times" his 76.8 (GG-11), and concludes:

> "we can account even for the highest end of the mutational differences with a model that assumes that selection was not involved at all." (gg1@02:02:07, GG-12)

The window ends as the host begins reading Day's counter on parallel fixation and neutral theory (gg1@02:04:14), which is the next window.

## 2. The maths, located and rebuilt

Two models meet. Day's (via the slides) is F_max = t_div · d / (g · G_f) (M-001): a window divided by an observed generations-per-fixation figure, at d = 1. It has no mutation rate, population size or selection coefficient; Day's side says G_f is a measured total throughput that already contains them (M-713). Hancock's is the neutral null: supply 2Nμ per site times fixation probability 1/(2N) gives k = μ, so the expected count of fixed differences over two lineages is 2 T μ L (M-209, M-212). It leaves out selection by design, the time a new neutral mutation takes to fix (M-914, M-917), and the ancestral polymorphism inside an observed divergence (B5c, R4 B4a).

## 3. What holds

- **The derivation of k = μ.** Hancock's Taylor step is exact in the limit (M-919) and the result is the library's resolved M-209; Day himself conceded the cancellation (M-209, 2026-08-27). The claim file B5 scores it internal holds, fidelity accurate.
- **The model has no mutation rate and no selection coefficient** (GG-24, GG-25). Literal and accurate (G2h). It means the formula cannot tell selected from neutral fixations: the 1,400 counts all of them (M-713).
- **The supply gap between E. coli and humans.** "Because our genome is much bigger and our mutation rate is much faster, we are going to fix many mutations every single generation, unlike bacteria, which don't." (gg1@01:54:43). The per-genome neutral supply ratio is 93,659 (M-007). A G_f measured in E. coli carries E. coli's supply (A5c).
- **The 205M is bases, not events.** "I'm going to assume this number is also including a gap divergences" (gg1@01:59:18) agrees with Day's own "Yoo reported a total genomic difference of 410 million base pairs." (Q120), halved (Q121). On events the count is about 21M per lineage; 205M is about 9.7x it (A3x, supported).
- **The no-variation prediction, as a conditional.** A strictly serial origin-and-fix model leaves almost no standing variation (GG-13); real site frequency spectra do not look like that. B6c and G2c score it supported as a conditional.
- **Fixation does run in parallel in real populations** (gg1@01:40:27). Day agrees: Q125 says the rate "INCLUDES parallel fixation". The dispute is whether G_f already holds the overlap, not whether overlap exists.
- **The slide's arithmetic and its framing.** 180 and 204,999,820 reproduce. The slide calls the 1,400 a rate "observed fixing in parallel" (gg1@01:28:09) and says twice that the 205 million are "in the human lineage" (gg1@01:26:23, 01:26:49), so the presented case was internally consistent on both points that Hancock attacks.
- **d = 1 is right to drop here.** "it's just one in his equation anyway" (gg1@01:30:13): the 2nd-edition 180 uses d = 1 (M-110). Day's later d = 0.80 would give 201,600 generations (evo009@L54).
- **Hancock corrects himself on the record.** The edit insert concedes the diploid genome size (GG-05, rule SC); he calls his own method "back of the napkin" (GG-16) and says he does not know where the 205M comes from rather than guessing.

## 4. What fails

### Errors (rule R1: correcting it moves the stated conclusion or a downstream number by >25%, or flips it)

- **"The fastest rate of mutational fixation ever observed" (slide, gg1@01:27:24; Day's own Q91).** Point-mutator LTEE lines run at 43-183 generations per fixation, and Day's own 3.0 uses 105 for mutators (M-711). Correcting G_f to 183 moves the 180 to 1,377 (+665%; M-711). Past 25%. Day's stated conclusion (180 ≪ 205 million) survives the correction. Already scored in the Nesslig20 review.
- **Hancock, E. coli μ "something like 1 e to the -11" (gg1@01:54:04).** The LTEE measured 8.9e-11 (M-007). Correcting the input moves his "22,000 generations to fix one mutation" to 2,439, −88.9% (M-919). Past 25%. His conclusion, that far less than one neutral mutation fixes per generation in E. coli and many in humans, survives (4.1e-4 < 1 < 38.4). The input is uncited (rule F); claim file A5c has fidelity unverifiable.
- **GG-11 on Day's per-lineage 205M** (reading-dependent, §7). If the 205 million are per human lineage, as the slide says, the needed supply is 813.5 per generation, not 407 (+100%), 10.6x the 76.8 rather than "five times", and 21.2x the haploid SNV supply (M-919). On Hancock's own reading of 205M as the human-chimp total ("that's the number that he showed as the difference between humans and chimps", gg1@01:59:05) the 407 holds. B5c already records this.
- **The 38 million match** (gg1@01:56:42-01:57:10; existing B5c verdict). On the haploid SNV basis Hancock declares live ("It's based on single nucleotide differences", gg1@01:57:50) the post-split count is 19.35M, 1.81-2.07x short of the 35-40M he cites (M-919). The remainder is ancestral polymorphism (B5c, R4 B4a: about 15M), which he does not mention. The wider conclusion (a neutral null suffices for the SNVs) survives with that term; the stated match does not.
- **Hosts: no other error found.** The remaining numbers reproduce.

### Non-sequiturs and hidden premises (rules N, U)

- **Serial from the formula's form (GG-01, gg1@01:32:47).** Implicit premise: K, the 1,400, is the time one allele takes from origin to fixation. The slide read four minutes earlier calls it a rate "observed fixing in parallel" (gg1@01:28:09), and Day's Q125 says the same. On that reading 1,400 is a mean spacing between completions and says nothing about overlap (M-115: 360 cars in 3,600 s is 10 s per car while ten are on the road). Against the book's Appendix A, which says "fixation must be sequential" (G2g q1), the premise holds, but the captions do not show that text on the slides. The claim file G2 scores it holds, fidelity partial.
- **"that's because it's assuming that they're going one at a time" (gg1@01:42:05).** From "no mutation rate" to "one at a time" does not follow: a measured throughput can contain any overlap without a μ term (M-706, M-713).
- **The no-variation prediction against Day (GG-13, gg1@01:34:08).** Valid as a conditional; it touches MITTENS only on the serial reading (G2c fidelity partial).
- **GG-12 mixes units.** The 407 (or 813.5) is a count of mutation events per generation; the 205M it is set against is a count of bases (M-011). "species with mutation rates of this amount" (gg1@02:01:58) is uncited (rule F). On events the requirement is about 21M per lineage (A3x), so the a fortiori argument is unnecessary and, as built, not like for like. Hancock himself calls the 205M "wrong" (gg1@02:03:36).
- **The insert's rescue swaps basis.** It keeps 76 by halving an all-variant de novo count (gg1@01:53:30), while the live comparison is with SNV divergence on an SNV rate (gg1@01:57:50). M-108; Day's post relays Duffy: "You can’t validate a SNP-only calculation with a number that includes large structural variation." (evo008@L83). B5c scores this N3, non-sequitur.
- **Relayed slide lines (rule U), noted, not scored as Day's own:** "the mathematical impossibility of the theory of evolution by natural selection" (gg1@01:28:43) needs two hidden premises the window disputes, that G_f bounds selection-driven fixation (M-713) and that 205M counts fixation events (M-011, A3x).

## 9. Critic-side steelman

The window's central result stands: neutral supply alone produces fixed differences at the mutation rate, k = μ, and Day has conceded the cancellation (M-209). That rate is enormous beside the slide's: 76.8 fixations per generation against 180 in 252,000, a ratio of 107,520, still 53,760 on the corrected haploid basis (M-919). The reason is the one Hancock gives: the 1,400 is an E. coli number, and per genome E. coli's neutral supply is 93,659 times smaller than a human's (M-007). Correcting his E. coli input strengthens this rather than weakening it. At the measured 8.9e-11 the bacterial neutral clock is 2,439 generations, close to the LTEE's 1,322-1,400, so 37-54% of the LTEE throughput is the mutation clock, which scales with supply (M-916). Day's formula has no μ and no selection term (G2h), so it cannot say which process its 180 limits; that is exactly what "this is not even necessarily a measure of selection" means. Hancock's suspicion that the 205M counts gap divergences is right in Day's own words, "410 million base pairs" (Q120). On events the requirement is about 21M per lineage, 9.7x smaller (A3x, supported), and that is the number a neutral model has to meet. Read as SNV supply against SNV divergence, the null gives 19.35M post-split and the remaining ~15M is ancestral polymorphism (B5c, R4 B4a), so the neutral account of the single-nucleotide differences survives with the term he omitted. The serial reading is not invented: Day's book says "Therefore fixation must be sequential" (G2g q1), and the claim file G2 scores the critique internal holds against that text. The no-variation prediction is the right test of such a model, and the audit's own runs support it as a conditional (B6c, G2c). The factor of two is correct as stated about the 180: it counts one lineage, and Hancock read 205M as the human-chimp difference (gg1@01:59:05). He corrected his genome size on the record, called his estimate "back of the napkin", and said where he was unsure. The slide meanwhile misstates its own premise: 1,400 is not the fastest rate observed, since point-mutator lines run at 43-183 generations per fixation (M-711; Day's own Q91).

## 10. Bottom line

Every number in the window reproduces on its speaker's own inputs, apart from three small roundings. Hancock's neutral derivation (k = μ) is exact and settled, and his point that a bacterial throughput carries bacterial supply is the strongest maths in the segment (M-007, A5c). His two attacks on the slide's own case, seriality and the factor of two, answer a serial, two-lineage reading. The slide he was shown frames the rate as parallel and the 205 million as per lineage, and the captions do not show Duffy presenting the Appendix A chain. Against that chain, which is in Day's book, the serial critique holds. His 38 million match double-counts on the single-nucleotide basis he declares; the ancestral term he omits restores it. His closing a fortiori sets an event rate against a base-pair count. Day may fairly take the slide's consistency, the misreadings of the comparator and the rate, and the corrected numbers (19.35M, 813.5, 2,439). The critics may fairly take k = μ, the 93,659x supply gap, bases against events, and the false "fastest ever" premise. New maths is limited to arithmetic (M-919); the questions are M-706, M-012, M-011 and M-103, all already in the library.
